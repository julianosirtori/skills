# Generating names

How to produce candidates that are brandable and likely to be free. Read this
before the first batch.

## Contents

- Yield by strategy (what was measured)
- The strategies, best yield first
- Making a name fit without describing the product
- Sound and spelling across languages
- Batch protocol

## Yield by strategy

Measured in the reference run (~870 names, .com):

| Strategy | Example | .com free |
|---|---|---|
| Two known words in an unusual combination | plazabuzz, kioskbuzz | **~40%** |
| Root + ending, generated in bulk | 17 roots × 22 endings | ~22%, but almost all weak |
| Invented word from a Latin root | ovato, furora | ~0% (.ai sometimes free) |
| Person's name with "hey"/"meet" | heymira, meetnova | ~0% |
| Single word or obvious compound | postcrew, buzzloop | ~0% |

The end-to-end test of this skill confirmed the pattern. Unusual
combinations had 55–57% of .com free across three batches. Single and
invented words had 0% (15 of 44 had get-/try- free).

So: if the user needs a .com, most of the effort goes into unusual
combinations. If a non-.com extension (.ai, .app, .io) or a modified .com
(get-, try-, -hq) is acceptable, single words and invented words come back.
Notion started on notion.so, Linear on linear.app, and Slack on slack.com
only after buying it.

## The strategies, best yield first

### 1. Unusual combination of two known words

Mailchimp, Dropbox, Snapchat, Airtable, Coinbase, Duolingo (blend), Patreon.

- **Pair across worlds.** Take one word from the product's world, or from the
  feeling it promises, and one from a distant world. Kiosk + buzz works because
  a kiosk is not where buzz is expected.
- **Use concrete nouns.** Things you can picture (kite, harbor, lantern,
  pebble, plaza) carry imagery and help visual identity. Abstract words
  (solution, smart, hub, pro) add nothing and are taken everywhere.
- **Prefer rhythm and sound repetition.** Alliteration, rhyme and stress
  patterns make it stick: PayPal, Kickstarter, Mailchimp. Say the name aloud;
  if it stumbles, drop it.
- **Keep it short.** Two or three syllables in total is ideal. Four is the
  limit unless the rhythm carries it.
- **Avoid accidental words at the join.** Check the seam for a third word:
  "therapist" famously hides "the rapist", and "expertsexchange" splits
  badly. Read the name with the words split in a different place.
- **Generate fast.** Build two columns of 20–30 words each (A: the product's
  world and feeling, B: unexpected nouns) and combine them. Write only the
  pairs you would say out loud, then check them all.

### 2. Arbitrary real word

Apple, Slack, Notion, Linear, Amazon, Oracle, Stripe.

A real word with no link to the category. It's strong in trademark terms and
memorable because it is familiar. The .com is almost never free, so this
strategy suits users who accept another extension, a modifier, or a purchase.
Look for words that are concrete, short, positive in every market language,
and that carry the brand's feeling (Slack: ease; Stripe: clean lines).

### 3. Evocative word or metaphor

Nike (victory), Patagonia (wild places), Amazon (scale), Kindle (to light).

Start from the promise or the feeling, not the feature. List them: speed,
calm, growth, craft, trust, play. For each, mine a semantic field: nature,
weather, astronomy, mythology, music, sport, navigation, places, animals,
materials, crafts. Then pick words whose image carries the feeling. These
pair well with strategy 1 when the single word is taken (harbor → harborkite).

### 4. Invented word

Kodak, Hulu, Zillow, Spotify, Vercel, Figma, Asana.

- **Root-based.** Take Latin or Greek roots tied to the feeling (lumen, velox,
  nova, ova), then cut, blend or change the ending (-a, -o, -ly, -io, -ify,
  -era).
- **Sound-first.** Build the name from consonant-vowel syllables that sound
  the way the brand feels (see sound symbolism below).
- **Bulk generation.** Roots × endings gives volume (about 22% free in the
  reference run), but most results are weak. Use it to find seeds, then
  curate hard.

Invented words have the strongest trademark potential and a clean search
slate. They also have the least meaning on day one, so they need more
marketing to fill them. The .com is usually taken for short ones; .ai and
.app are often free.

### 5. Blend (portmanteau)

Pinterest (pin + interest), Instagram (instant + telegram), Groupon, Netflix.

Both parts must stay recognizable. If people can't hear the two words inside,
it's just an invented word that is hard to spell.

### 6. Altered spelling

Lyft, Flickr, Fiverr, Tumblr.

This frees up taken words, but it fails the radio test by design: everyone
who hears it types it wrong. Use it only when the brand can afford to teach
the spelling, and also secure the correct spelling's domain if possible.

### 7. Person or character name

heymira, meetnova, Oscar, Alexa.

It feels warm and personal, which suits assistants and companion products.
It's almost never free as a .com, even with hey/meet/ask, and personal names
are crowded in search. Use it only with a non-.com extension or a strong
reason.

## Making a name fit without describing the product

The name doesn't describe the product, so fit has to come from somewhere
else:

- **The feeling the brand promises.** A scheduling tool promises calm, so
  calm imagery fits (harbor, lantern) even though it says nothing about
  scheduling.
- **The personality.** Playful brands can use bouncy sounds and odd pairings
  (Mailchimp). Premium or expert brands need restraint: one strong word,
  clean sounds (Stripe, Linear).
- **The audience's world.** References the audience recognizes and likes.
- **A story to tell.** If you can explain the name in one sentence that
  makes people smile or nod ("Kiosk because it's the corner stand where your
  neighborhood finds you"), the marketing team has something to work with.

For each name on the shortlist, write that sentence. If you can't, the name
probably doesn't fit.

## Sound and spelling across languages

**Sound symbolism.** These associations hold across most languages:

- Front vowels (i, e) feel small, quick, light and precise.
- Back vowels (o, u) feel big, solid, round and calm.
- Plosives (p, t, k, b, d, g) feel energetic, crisp and decisive.
- Sonorants (l, m, n, r) feel soft, smooth and friendly.
- Fricatives (s, f, v, z) feel fast and fluid.

Match the sounds to the personality.

**Names that travel.** Consonant-vowel syllables (Ka-ro, Lu-mo, Zi-va) are
pronounced almost the same in Portuguese, Spanish, English and Italian.
Watch the sounds that change between languages, especially when the markets
mix Portuguese and English:

- **Initial r** is an h sound in Brazilian Portuguese ("Rio" → "Hio").
- **th and w** are hard for many Portuguese and Spanish speakers.
- **ch and x** are pronounced differently in each language.
- **Final e and i**: English speakers say "-ee", Portuguese speakers often
  read a final e as "-ee" too, and Spanish speakers say "-eh".
- **ão, nh, lh and ç** don't exist in English and can't go in a domain.

**Spelling.** One obvious spelling for whoever hears it. Avoid double letters
that people drop (Fiverr), letters that sound alike (c/k/q, s/z, i/y), and
silent letters. If an accented brand spelling matters (Postência), it can't
be the domain: the domain loses the accent and people won't type it.

**Length.** On average, shorter is more memorable and more taken. Four to
eight letters is the sweet spot for brandability; up to 10–12 is fine for
two-word combinations with good rhythm.

## Batch protocol

1. Pick the strategy with the best remaining yield for the required extension.
2. Write 40–80 names to `candidates/NN-<strategy>.txt`, dropping obvious
   failures as you go (unpronounceable, forbidden-language word, competitor
   sound-alike).
3. Check them: `check_domains.sh -t <main tld> < candidates/NN-<strategy>.txt > results/NN-<strategy>.tsv`.
4. Record the yield: free ÷ checked.
5. Read the free names. Put the promising ones in `shortlist.md` and look for
   patterns worth mining (same structure, same seed word).
6. If the yield is under ~5%, or the free names are all weak, switch
   strategy. Otherwise, do another batch around what worked.

Never reuse a name already checked: compare with the earlier `results/*.tsv`
before writing a new batch (`cut -f1 results/*.tsv | cut -d. -f1 | sort -u`).
